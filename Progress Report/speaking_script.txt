==================================================
Our goal is simple.
We build a prototype that shows contactless detection is technically possible with local tools.
The thesis promises these things.
We train and validate the detection models. The software around them is required, but it is secondary.

SLIDE 4: System at a glance
==================================================
Let me walk through the system from left to right.
A video comes in. It can be a recorded clip or a stream. We cap it to 720p to keep it fast.
Step one, detect. YOLO finds vehicles and people in every frame.
Step two, track. DeepSORT gives each vehicle one ID. If a track breaks, we stitch it back.
Then the rules and the specialists work. Zones and geometry judge the movement. Helmet and cabin models look at crops.
Step three, plate. We find the plate, make it sharper, and read it with two-pass voting.
At the end, we make a ticket with the violation, the plate, the track ID, and photos with "why" boxes.
Around it, we have four apps.
The backend runs locally. It uses FastAPI, SQLite, and ONNX on the GPU.
The enforcer dashboard is for review and training.
The citizen app is for notices and appeals.
The visualizer teaches each violation to non-technical people. It is a teaching aid, not part of the detection claim.
One design choice is important.
We use specialists on cropped vehicles, not one big model on the whole frame.
That keeps it fast on a 6 GB laptop GPU, and we can retrain each model alone.

SLIDE 11: Legal context: non-contact apprehension
==================================================
The panel may ask: "Is this even allowed?"
Here is the context.
In August 2022, the Supreme Court issued a TRO. It stopped the No Contact Apprehension Policy of the MMDA and some cities.
In May 2025, the TRO was partly lifted for the MMDA on major roads like EDSA and C5. City ordinances were not covered.
In mid-2026, the Court dismissed the petitions as moot.
It did not say that the policy is valid. Reports give different dates, so we must confirm the date.
The petitioners raised privacy, conditions like blocking registration renewal, and due process for owners who were not the driver.
For our thesis, the answer is clear.
We study the detection technology, not the legal power to fine.
A human reviews every flag, and owners are not notified automatically.
Masked personal data and plate correction answer the privacy concern, at least in part.
Our source is news reports. Before quoting, confirm the case numbers and the decision date on the Supreme Court website.

SLIDE 14: How a ticket is decided
==================================================
This slide answers: "How do you control false positives?"
Six steps.
One, never trust one frame. Model checks vote over the last 10 looks. One wrong guess cannot make a ticket.
Two, judge fairly. A plate look only counts if the vehicle is big enough and its class is settled.
Three, decide at the end. No plate is only a suspicion while the car is on screen.
It becomes a ticket only if no plate was found when the car leaves. Photos of cleared suspicions are deleted.
Four, show why. The evidence photos have boxes: the zone, the vehicle, and the part that caused the flag.
Five, a human decides. The reviewer confirms, rejects, or corrects.
Six, learn from it. Rejected tickets and corrections go to the harvest inbox for the next training round.
So we have four independent layers: voting, fair-look rules, end-of-track decisions, and human review.

SLIDE 16: Measured: the model input mismatch
==================================================
This is a measurement, not an opinion.
Same model, same photos. Only the way we prepare the picture changes.
Today, the app uses BGR, stretched, and only the bottom 40 percent of the vehicle. The plate is found in only 7 percent.
If we only fix the colors to RGB, it is 11 percent.
RGB plus letterbox, still bottom 40 percent, is 29 percent.
RGB plus letterbox, on the whole vehicle, is 85 percent.
Why? Only 51 percent of plates sit in the bottom 40 percent of the vehicle box. That is the only area the app searched.
Also, from 28 real "no plate" tickets, 18 percent had a plate the model can find when fed properly.
The fix has three pieces: convert BGR to RGB, letterbox instead of stretching, and search the whole vehicle.
Important: the fix is queued, not applied yet.
And the 85 percent is on the plate set's own domain. It is not on Zamboanga footage.

SLIDE 26: The AI architecture in one picture
==================================================
This is the whole AI system in one picture.
There are four kinds of model, and one part that is not AI.
First, the detector. It finds vehicles and people in each frame.
Second, the tracker. It gives each vehicle one ID, so we don't count the same car twice.
Third, the specialists. They look at a small crop: helmet, cabin, or plate.
Fourth, the plate reader. It makes the plate sharper, then reads the letters and numbers.
The last block is not AI. It is plain rules and votes. Doon nagdedesisyon kung may violation.
Notice: the detector and the three specialists are the same small model, YOLOv8 nano.
Each one has about 3 million parameters. So it can run on a laptop.
The detector sees the whole frame at 640 by 640. The specialists see crops at 320 by 320.
We fine-tuned the four YOLO models ourselves, starting from COCO weights.
The tracker and the plate reader are pretrained. We did not train them ourselves.
For plate reading, we need about 500 labelled plates. We have 42.

SLIDE 27: Inside the detector
==================================================
Now, what happens inside the detector?
Step one: we resize the picture to 640 by 640. We do not stretch it. We add padding.
Step two: the backbone. It turns pixels into features. First edges, then parts, then whole objects.
Step three: the neck. It mixes three picture sizes, so we can find small and big objects.
Step four: the head. It checks 8,400 grid cells. Each cell gives one box and one score per class.
Where is 8,400 from? 80 times 80, plus 40 times 40, plus 20 times 20.
Step five: the filter. We throw away scores below 0.45. Then we remove duplicate boxes.
How does it learn? We start from a model that already knows 80 common objects. This is called transfer learning.
Then we train it on our photos. Each round: it guesses, we measure the mistakes, it adjusts a little.
There are three kinds of mistake: box, class, and dfl. Remember them. They are in the training graph.
Why YOLO? One pass over the picture gives every box. It is fast enough for video.
Why nano? 3 million parameters. It runs in real time on our laptop GPU.

==================================================
SLIDE 28: Tracking and plate reading
==================================================
Two small stories here.
First, the tracker, DeepSORT. Its question is: "Is this the same car as one second ago?"
It guesses where the car should be now. Then it matches new boxes to old cars.
It uses position, and also how the car looks. The look is a fingerprint of 512 numbers.
A car needs three good frames before it gets an ID.
If it disappears, we keep its ID for up to 90 frames.
Second story: the plate. First we find the plate inside the vehicle. We cut it with a little padding.
We keep up to 40 crops per vehicle.
Then Real-ESRGAN makes it bigger and sharper. Then EasyOCR reads the text.
We fix common mistakes by position. In a PH plate, the first three are letters and the last four are numbers.
So a zero in a letter place becomes O.
Finally, we vote across all the crops. The text with the most reads wins.
Why vote? One blurry frame can read B as 8. Many frames together are more reliable.
That is why one car becomes one ticket with one plate.

SLIDE 29: How the models were trained
==================================================
How did we train the models?
The method is transfer learning. We start from COCO-pretrained YOLOv8 nano weights.
The hardware is an NVIDIA RTX 3050 laptop GPU with 6 GB.
The software is Ultralytics, PyTorch, ONNX Runtime, and Python 3.12.
We train up to 100 epochs. Vehicles are 30. Batch size is 16.
We stop early after 30 epochs without improvement.
We use augmentation: mosaic, color shifts, flips, and small rotation.
The best weights are exported to ONNX and registered in the model catalog.
Why this design?
Specialists on crops are smaller, faster, and we can retrain them one at a time.
Every run keeps its settings and its scores, so it is reproducible.
We can pause and resume, because the GPU is shared with other work.
I must mention one thing before you ask.
Helmet, cabin, and plate were trained at 640 pixels. But the pipeline feeds them 320 pixel crops.
So we must re-check the scores at 320 before we call them field numbers.

SLIDE 33: The learning loop
==================================================
This answers: "How will accuracy improve beyond public data?"
Mistakes become training data.
One, analyze. We run a clip in the Forensic Analyzer.
Two, review. A person confirms, rejects, or corrects the plate.
Three, collect. The harvest tool copies uncertain and rejected pictures, with the model's own boxes.
Four, check. One picture at a time. Enter means the box is right. Drag to draw. N means nothing here.
Five, train. Accept into the dataset, retrain, and deploy.
Six, benchmark. Same clips, compare with the previous model.
Each ticket type teaches a different model.
A "no plate" ticket teaches the plate model. A "no helmet" ticket teaches the helmet model.
A corrected plate text teaches the plate reader. A merge or "different car" decision teaches the car matcher.
There are safeguards.
Benchmark clips can be protected from harvesting.
The test split is never written to.
And nothing enters a dataset without a human check.

SLIDE 34: Evaluation plan
==================================================
This answers: "How will you know it works?"
Please note, the runner is not built yet.
The protocol has five steps.
One, a clip registry. For each clip, we store the zones, the expected events, and the pass criteria.
Two, ground truth. Two people count the violations independently. We settle and record disagreements.
Three, two sets of clips. Tuning clips, where we can adjust thresholds. And held-out test clips, which we touch once.
Four, run with fixed settings. Score each event as true positive, false positive, or false negative.
Five, report precision, recall, and logging accuracy, with confidence intervals and a failure analysis.
We will also compare.
YOLOv8n versus YOLO11n versus YOLO26n on the same clips and rules.
We will do ablations: with and without plate enhancement, DeepSORT versus ByteTrack, and bottom 40 percent versus whole vehicle.
We report plate reading separately from plate detection.
Precision is: of the tickets raised, how many were real.
Recall is: of the real violations, how many got a ticket.
And I want to ask: how many events per violation do you consider enough?

SLIDE 35: Limits and risks
==================================================
This is what we cannot claim yet. I say it before you ask.
Evidence. No benchmark on field footage yet.

Data. No photo is known to be from Zamboanga.

System. The app feeds the models differently from training. The fix is queued.

Outcome. Tickets do not reach owners yet. There is no plate-to-owner registry.

We found and measured our own failure modes.
What do I avoid saying?
"99 percent accurate". "Works on Philippine roads". "Ready for deployment". And "detects all 13 violations" without saying that field measurement is pending.

SLIDE 38: Questions: method
==================================================
Question 6. Why a cascade of specialists, not one detector with every class?
Small models on small crops are fast on a laptop GPU. We can retrain and compare them one at a time.
The trade-off: errors compound. A missed vehicle means no helmet check.
We will test it with an ablation against one multi-class model, if time allows.
Question 7. Why YOLOv8n, and why compare YOLO11 and YOLO26?
Nano runs in real time on a 6 GB GPU.
All three generations are supported. So the comparison changes only the detector.
Question 8. Most violations are geometry rules. Where is the AI?
The AI is in perception: detection, tracking appearance, plate reading, helmet and cabin checks.
The violation itself is a traffic definition. Counterflow is, by definition, movement against the legal direction.
So a rule on a learned track is the correct tool.
We tune the thresholds on clips, and we test the rules with scripted tracks.
Question 9. How do you deal with the same car getting several IDs?
We found a real bug and fixed it. We added a stitcher.
Reviewers can merge or separate cars, and those decisions become ReID data.
We will report ID switches per clip.
Question 10. How do you control false positives?
Four layers: votes, fair-look rules, end-of-track decisions, and human review.
Our rule tests include traps, like a car waiting on a stop line.

SLIDE 47: D. Glossary (appendix)
==================================================
This slide is for panel members outside computer vision.
mAP at 50. How well boxes match real objects. A box is correct if it overlaps by at least half. 1.00 is perfect.
mAP at 50 to 95. The same, but stricter. Low values mean loose boxes.
Precision, or "trust". Of the things flagged, how many were real.
Recall, or "coverage". Of the real things, how many were found.
Validation versus test. Validation is checked during training and picks the best epoch. Test is one final exam.
Letterbox. Resizing without stretching, with padding on the sides.
ReID. A small model that tells if two photos show the same vehicle.
Transfer learning. Start from a model that knows everyday objects, then teach it our classes.
